(Botanist here)

C4 less efficient than C3? Kinda.

In C3 plants, every cell with chlorophyll photosynthesizes. They top out around 30% of maximum sunlight.

C4 straps a turbocharger onto C3. Some cells use photosynthesis to make 4-carbon compounds that they ship next door to the C3 cells, to keep their CO2 concentrations up. Thing is, C3 is actually really good at fixing carbon into carbs, but the reaction starts going wrong if there's not enough CO2 in the cell. C4 fixes that. Since C3 tops out at such low light levels, it doesn't matter if there are a bunch of C4 cells pumping carbon into the C3 cells to keep them photosynthesizing at light levels where they'd normally max out.

Obviously, in a place like the UK, light is limiting, so C4 isn't useful. Similarly, if something else is throttling photosynthesis--low temperatures for example--C4 isn't useful.

All else being equal, more CO2 in the atmosphere will make life a bit easier for C3 plants, but the advantage gets lost when it's hotter.

Thing is, climate change only indirectly affects light, because how much light hits a place is about orbital trigonometry (angle of the sun on a given day relative to the plant), plus relative thickness of the atmosphere (slant light goes through more air), plus cloudiness.

This last is where climate change might matter. There's some modeling evidence that climate change makes high latitudes less cloudy, and there's real evidence that the poles are heating up faster than the equator, on a relative basis. But when you pound all the factors together, C4 plants will do better at lower latitudes, C3 plants will do better at higher latitudes, and the real problem is finding crops that will deal with the available climatic and biological regimes in a given place in a given season. That's not easy, unfortunately.

What do we do with the "mining waste"

Reprocessing is often a partial solution, there's a couple of gold mines in Australia that are getting a decade or so out of re-doing old mine tailings with new technology. I've read a report saying that getting rare earths out of tailings from other mine types is possible but with China dominating supply it's not economic. OTOH those gold tailings weren't economic until recently. What we need in Oz is a use for huge amounts of arsenic :)

With old PV panels the question is often whether it's cheaper to leave them in place or pay to replace them, and that's essentially a political question. If the cost of disposing of the old panels goes up, or labour costs rise, leaving them out for longer becomes more attractive. For small scale users leaving them up until they completely fail is generally worth while, often depending on technical factors like whether the surviving string has enough voltage to be useful (commonly the output needs to be, say 300-650V and at time of install the system is at the upper limit so you can lose half the panels and still have useful output).

Recycling panels is also increasingly lucrative. The easy part is the glass and that used to be low value but as that glass becomes increasingly specialised and demand has increased, the price for second hand glass has risen to the point where its a saleable commodity. The silver has always been valuable, but not enough to pay the entire recycling cost. Ditto the aluminium frames. But greater supply of second hand panels means modern automated recycling plants are now viable.

Cheap solar energy also makes a bunch of things viable that were not before, from mining seawater for sodium to low population density cellphone networks.

(this applies to a bunch of "but what about the old ones" objections, especially lithium batteries. But even plastic wind turbine blades are now recyclable, at least somewhat, and that seems likely to increase)
